Before continuing on this road let's first clarify what these models are since the majority of the AI field uses them as a basis.

Models are in itself functions. If we take for example the most simple function you had at school which was: `y = a * x + b`. In this case the value `x`  is what the model is given as input, this is usually a number representing the information that we are working with. Then `a` and `b` are the `model parameters`, you will often find them called the `model weights` . You can think of this function like some blueprint or architecture of a building, and then the weights are different parameters that can be changed to actually make the building suit our usecase better, maybe we want to make it more robust to windstorms so instead of wood we will use metal, or maybe we want to make it cheaper, so we will use cement instead of that, maybe we need the building to be an office space so then we will make it have more separate rooms, and this entire process of `fitting` the model to our usecase is usually called training, we are training the model to `fit` our usecase better (I am emphasizing fit since it is usually used in the technical domain of AI in the training process). But how do we actually find the right parameters for our use case, how does training actually work? 

So training usually depends on the usecase that we are talking about, there are two main possibilities to think of, supervised and unsupervised training. Let's put this through an example, supervised learning is when you have the task at hand, and you are also given the solution, so you're goal is to find the right parameters which given the task they will provide you the solution that you already know for that task. In the usecase of unsupervised learning, you don't actually know what is the solution, you just have the task at hand and your goal is to form some kind of solution from that, I know it might sound confusing at first, so here are some real world examples.
- It's important for marketplaces to understand their customers and how they behave, and by understanding this customers you could afterwards make better deals or proposals to stimulate buying. That's where unsupervised learning comes into place, we take the data that we collect from the history of the marketplace, what each user bought, when, how much of it, and then feed this into a model that could afterwards create some group of clients based on their activity.
- Often times people want to buy or sell a house and sometimes the price could represent a gray zone when it comes to apartments or houses, that's why there are use cases of models used for pricing houses. This is a perfect usecase for supervised learning where we can use market history to understand given the parameters of the house, what would be a good price for it.

For the process of training we always need data, be this unsupervised or supervised task, we always need need to work with data and preferably good one. To clarify what good data means I will give you a simple example, when you teach a student a school how to solve equations you would probably not give him a wrong example of how the equation was solve, that would make him confused. The same way when working with models, if we want to have good results we have to make sure that the data we provide is accurate for the problem that we aim to solve.

Now we have the data, so what do we do next with it. When we are at school the learning process usually looks like this, you are taught something, then you have some practice exercises around it, then you might have some flash tests to check your understanding and at the end you have your final exam on the subject. It's kind of the same with training a model. You first have you're training period, where you first interact with the concept and go through as many practice examples as possible to get a good grasp of what is happening. After you've done some good iterations you want to validate that information, so you do some flash tests which are similar to what you have been doing so far, and then this cycle might happen a few times till you understood well the entire concept. And when the final day comes you go to the test and show what you're really made of. The thing is in both the training, validation and test parts you never have repeating exercises, the concept remains the same and some parameters change, but you never have the same thing, since that would be cheating, and you wouldn't learn to solve the actual exercise, you would just learn what are the answers like memorizing a poem, which is not our goal.

As per the reference above, let's assume that we are working on an AI project, and we have been given some dataset to work with, the logic that we want to follow is to split the data in 3 parts, training, validation and testing. Keep in mind that those do not have to be 3 equal parts, actually it is recommended that the majority of your data goes towards the training part, but never forget your validation and testing set. What instead should be equal is the distribution of examples. In simple words, you know when at school you were taught something in class and then given something completely different at the test, that we want to avoid. We have to make sure that all the use cases that we want our model to be able to solve should be present in all 3 sets of data, so that we could train well on them, validate and iterate, and last but not least test to ensure good performance around it.

Training itself is an iterative process. Looking from the supervised learning perspective the following is happening: 1. We start with some random parameters set for the model, we don't really care about those at the start since we will either way have to learn them, we then use the randomly set model to try and predict some answers from the training set, so the training set you could define as two parts, the input values, and the expected output, we take the input values and feed them to the model, which afterwards gets out some output information (based on the randomly generated parameters) and that is our answer. The next step is understanding how well or how bad was the prediction of the model, and for that we are using a cost function. In simple words the cost functions tells us how close is the predicted value to the expected value, the closer it is, the better and vice versa. Now the magic happens, we know how bad our model did, how do we actually update the model to make better predictions, and that's where Gradient Descent comes into place, Gradient Descent is an optimization algorithm that takes into consideration all the operations that were done by the model to reach the prediction and then uses the cost function to understand how well did it do to afterwards update the model weights (or model parameters) to improve the prediction. We are doing a full pass through the training dataset updating the model parameters and afterwards we come to the validation stage, where now we can stop the training and strictly use the model to make predictions, and the cost function can be used to see how well is the model doing at predicting things on the validation without any bias from the training data since it has already seen it before.

When a model starts memorizing the data instead of actually forming some logic behind it you're model is `overfitting`, when instead your model is not adapting to the data well and is not creating some kind of logical connection and is nowhere near memorizing it, you're model is `underfitting`. And if you didn't figure out already, we usually want our model to be in the middle of these too, the sweet spot of them both. Usually the best way of actually making the model understand and not memorize is to give him a lot of different examples so he could form a logic behind them, but there are a lot of edge cases here to consider.

We've been talking so far about different methods of training, but there are also different use cases of training, to give you a general understanding, anything that can be represented in a numerical format can be used to train a model. And everything can be represented in a numerical format! If you are not sure why numerical, it's because that's the only thing computers are able to understand.

So what are LLMs (Large Language Models)
I basically just told you what they are, but in a simple more common way, we talked about what models are, and that they can learn given data. At the end of the day all models process numerical information, but there are different ways in which you could represent data and then there are different ways in which you could architect this data to be learned. For example if you have a very muscular body of a human and you put it to lift heavy weights, it would probably do pretty well, then if you take the same muscular body and you put it at running a marathon you might find it struggling to perform that well. Same thing with models, some model architectures are really good when it comes to Image and Video processing but that does not imply that they will also be good when it comes to textual information. So Language Models have at their base the transformer architecture which showed really good results when it comes down to text processing. But let's take a step back from all of this, if we said at the start that models are just predicting information, then what are Language Models predicting you might ask yourself? And the answer is simple, words (or better said tokens but will get to that in a bit), the models are basically trying to solve the following task: Given the text "here usually goes all the text or discussion or information you provide to the LLM" what is the next probable word, and Language Models are doing this for every single word, one by one.

Now an interesting thing that this Language Models brought is that they were becoming increasingly better as engineers were giving them more data and making their architectures bigger overall, which is not something that you would have seen in previous architectures, as we talked previously there was usually a sweet spot everywhere. So that's how Language Models evolved from a few millions of parameters to now hundreds of billions of them, and just to give you a reminder, these parameters are basically the model weights that were learned during the process of training. Now to also clarify a thing there is also the word Large in Large Language Models, but throughtout the years it just became a comparison element since the previous models considered Large are quite small when put into perspective with the top models of today, and that's where Small Language Models (SLMs) came from, I think today's community usually refer to a model as an SLM when it can be run on a general purpose hardware like your laptop or your phone. And then the Large ones come when you would probably need a bit more resources for that, there is no clear common definition of the sizing/naming part that I am aware of at least, and considering that the technologies are getting better and better, which means we can run bigger models on them, there is definitely some confusion into that direction, though the majority of society would refer to them as LLMs.
